# Publication Brief

## Core public-interest problem

Africa's AI incident record is fragmented across fact-checks, newsrooms, company notices, court files and global incident databases. Fragmentation makes comparison difficult and can hide patterns that matter for governance, security and public accountability.

## Seed release findings

The initial AAIO release contains 17 source-traceable incidents across nine primary African countries. The seed is deliberately conservative and heavily reflects what is publicly visible: synthetic media, election/influence incidents, scams, conflict information, model behaviour and public-policy integrity failures.

That skew is itself a research finding. High-visibility synthetic-media cases are easier to document than internal failures in hospitals, banks, workplaces or government systems. A serious African incident-monitoring agenda therefore has to improve access to deployment evidence rather than simply count online deepfakes.

## What makes AAIO different

- It preserves source links and uncertainty instead of flattening every claim into a fact.
- It separates evidence confidence from impact severity.
- It keeps important non-qualifying automated-system cases on a watchlist rather than mislabelling them as AI incidents.
- It can export machine-readable records while remaining understandable to journalists, policy researchers and practitioners.
- It is designed to complement global repositories, not claim to replace them.

## Claims that should NOT be made

Do not say AAIO is comprehensive, representative of all African countries, endorsed by AIID/OECD/AU, or proof that one country has more AI incidents than another. Record counts are influenced by reporting visibility and research coverage.

## Suggested publication thesis

**Responsible AI needs institutional memory. Africa cannot learn systematically from AI failures that are never structured as evidence.**
