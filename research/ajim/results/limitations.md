# Limitations for the AAIO/AJIM analysis

1. **Purposive rather than exhaustive discovery.** The multilingual extension was deliberately constructed to test whether French-, Arabic- and Portuguese-language discovery surfaces evidence missed by the English-dominant core. It is not a census of African AI incidents and does not estimate true incidence rates.

2. **Small multilingual sample.** The multilingual layer contains 9 candidates, intentionally balanced at three records per target language. That design makes language comparison transparent but prevents population-level inference.

3. **Candidate/core separation.** All 9 multilingual records remain candidates. Their inclusion in the discovery analysis does not mean that they have been promoted into the 19-record core dataset.

4. **Source concentration.** The core is heavily linked to AIID: 17 of 19 records have populated AIID cross-references. The multilingual layer also relies on four fact-checking organisations, with AFP Factuel and Misbar contributing two thirds of the candidate set. Discovery-source concentration may shape what harms are visible.

5. **Translation process.** Multilingual records use machine-assisted curator translation/synthesis rather than certified or native-speaker translation. Translation uncertainty is recorded, but residual linguistic and contextual error remains possible.

6. **Public-evidence bias.** Incidents that generate public fact-checks, court records, regulator notices or journalism are easier to document than failures inside hospitals, banks, employers or government systems. Absence from AAIO is therefore not evidence of absence.

7. **Primary-country simplification.** Each record has a primary country even when an event has cross-border effects. Geographic counts therefore describe the project's indexing choice, not the full geographic reach of harms.

8. **Free-text source categories.** Secondary-source types are not yet fully normalized. Twelve distinct labels appear across 19 core records, so source-type diversity partly reflects labeling granularity.

9. **Taxonomy-loss interpretation.** The OECD and AIID mapping analysis measures semantic transferability from the AAIO schema. An unmapped field does not mean the underlying incident evidence is weak, and a direct mapping does not imply OECD compliance, endorsement or submission readiness.

10. **Severity non-equivalence.** AAIO's severity score is a project-specific analytic aid and is intentionally not converted into OECD severity. The resulting unmapped status for OECD criterion 10 is a design choice to avoid false precision.

11. **Post-freeze upstream changes.** A later duplicate check identified an existing AIID incident corresponding to AAIO-0019, but the frozen dataset retains its original null upstream identifier because the repository workflow requires an externally verified acceptance/merge outcome before altering the core record. The frozen statistics therefore remain reproducible from the recorded inputs.

12. **No causal comparison across languages.** The result that multilingual discovery added six previously unrepresented countries cannot establish that language alone caused under-documentation. Search strategy, source networks, editorial priorities and publication availability are plausible co-determinants.

13. **Single-curator coding.** AAIO coding was not independently double-coded for this study, so no inter-rater reliability statistic is reported. The paper must present this transparently and must not imply independent coder agreement.

14. **Manuscript-use boundary.** Repository prose is an analysis aid and reproducibility record. It should not be copied into a journal submission where the publisher requires author-written manuscript text.
